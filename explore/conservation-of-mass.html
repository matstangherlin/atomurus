Conservation of mass: why the total mass does not change in a reaction

Atoms are rearranged in a chemical reaction, never created or destroyed. See why the total mass of a closed system stays the same, why burning and rusting seem to break the rule, and how balanced equations follow from it.

Burn a log and only a little ash is left; leave an iron nail out in the rain and it gets heavier as it rusts. Mass seems to come and go in chemical changes — but when nothing can get in or out of the system, the total mass stays the same.

In this article you will see what the law of conservation of mass says, why it holds, why open containers seem to break it, and how it becomes the rule behind every balanced chemical equation. The masses in the worked examples are computed from molar masses by the same engine as the Atomurus calculators.

Quick definition In a closed system, the total mass of the substances before a chemical reaction is equal to the total mass after it. Atoms are rearranged into new substances; none are created or destroyed.

§ 01 The law of conservation of mass

The law states that matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. If you weigh everything that goes into a reaction and everything that comes out, the two totals agree, within the precision of the balance. The law is associated with the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier.

The important words are closed system: a container that lets no matter in or out. Energy may still cross its walls — a reaction can heat or cool the flask — but no gas escapes and no air comes in.

§ 02 Why mass is conserved

A chemical reaction breaks some bonds and forms others, so atoms end up in new combinations. The atoms themselves do not change: an atom of carbon remains an atom of carbon, with the same mass, whether it is in methane or in carbon dioxide.

Since every atom present before the reaction is still present after it, the sum of their masses is the same. The energy released or absorbed by a chemical reaction corresponds to a change in mass far too small to detect on any laboratory balance. Nuclear reactions, which change the atoms themselves, are a different matter and are not covered by this law in its chemical form.

§ 03 Open and closed systems

Most everyday observations that seem to contradict the law involve an open system. When wood burns, most of its mass leaves as carbon dioxide and water vapour, so the ash weighs much less than the log. If the gases are counted too, nothing is missing.

The opposite also happens. When iron rusts or steel wool burns, the solid takes oxygen from the air and gets heavier. The mass gained is exactly the mass of oxygen that reacted. In a sealed flask, with the air inside counted, the total would not change.

This is why experiments on conservation of mass are done in closed containers, or with every gas collected and weighed.

§ 04 Balanced equations are conservation of mass

A balanced chemical equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides. That is the law of conservation of mass written in symbols. Balancing means choosing the coefficients — the numbers in front of the formulas — never changing the subscripts, which would turn one substance into another.

Because each atom keeps its mass, a balanced equation also balances in grams. Using the molar masses, the mass of the reactants equals the mass of the products:

Example: 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O

  1. Mass of the reactants, for the amounts in the equation (in mol): 2 × M(H₂) = 2 × 2.016 g/mol = 4.032 g · 1 × M(O₂) = 1 × 31.998 g/mol = 31.998 g
  2. Mass of the products: 2 × M(H₂O) = 2 × 18.015 g/mol = 36.03 g
  3. Add up and compare both sides. reactants: 4.032 + 31.998 = 36.03 g · products: 36.03 g

Answer: 36.03 g = 36.03 g

Notice that the number of molecules changes — three on the left, two on the right — while the number of atoms of each element and the total mass do not.

§ 05 Working with masses

The same reasoning applies to any balanced equation. For the combustion of methane, the main component of natural gas:

Example: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O

  1. Mass of the reactants, for the amounts in the equation (in mol): 1 × M(CH₄) = 1 × 16.043 g/mol = 16.043 g · 2 × M(O₂) = 2 × 31.998 g/mol = 63.996 g
  2. Mass of the products: 1 × M(CO₂) = 1 × 44.009 g/mol = 44.009 g · 2 × M(H₂O) = 2 × 18.015 g/mol = 36.03 g
  3. Add up and compare both sides. reactants: 16.043 + 63.996 = 80.04 g · products: 44.009 + 36.03 = 80.04 g

Answer: 80.04 g = 80.04 g

It also lets you predict masses. When calcium carbonate is heated strongly, it decomposes into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. Starting from a known mass, the masses of the two products must add up to it:

Example: 10 g of CaCO₃ heated: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

  1. The amount of calcium carbonate. n = 10 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 0.09991 mol
  2. The equation gives 1 mol of each product per mol of CaCO₃. m(CaO) = 0.09991 mol × 56.077 g/mol = 5.603 g · m(CO₂) = 0.09991 mol × 44.009 g/mol = 4.397 g
  3. Add up the masses of the products. 5.603 g + 4.397 g = 10.00 g

Answer: 10.00 g = 10.00 g

If the carbon dioxide is allowed to escape, the solid left behind weighs less than the starting sample — by the mass of carbon dioxide released.

§ 06 Common misconceptions

"Burning makes matter disappear"

Burning turns solids and liquids into gases that leave the container. The matter is still there, as carbon dioxide and water vapour in the air. Collected and weighed, the products have the same mass as the fuel plus the oxygen it used.

"If mass is conserved, the number of molecules is too"

Atoms are conserved, molecules are not. In 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, three molecules become two. The four hydrogen atoms and the two oxygen atoms are all still there, so the mass is the same.

"Dissolving a solid makes its mass vanish"

When sugar dissolves in water it can no longer be seen, but the mass of the solution is the mass of the water plus the mass of the sugar. The particles have spread out; none have been lost.

"To balance an equation you can change the subscripts"

Changing a subscript changes the substance: H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, not water. Only the coefficients in front of the formulas may change, because they say how many particles of each substance take part.

§ 07 Key takeaways

  • In a closed system, the total mass before a chemical reaction equals the total mass after it.
  • Atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed; molecules can change in number.
  • Apparent gains or losses of mass come from gases entering or leaving an open system.
  • A balanced equation has the same atoms on both sides, so reactants and products also have the same mass.
  • Balance with coefficients, never by changing the subscripts of a formula.
Conclusion Conservation of mass is the bookkeeping rule of chemistry: every atom is accounted for. Combined with the mole and molar mass, it lets you predict how many grams of product a reaction can give — the subject of stoichiometry.
