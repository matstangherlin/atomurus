Solutions and concentration: moles per litre, preparing and diluting a solution

A solution is a solute spread evenly through a solvent, and its concentration says how much solute is in each litre. Learn how to calculate the mass to weigh, how to make the solution up to the mark and why diluting changes the concentration but not the amount of solute.

Sea water, a cup of coffee, the saline drip in a hospital: most of the chemistry around us happens in solutions. To work with them you need to know not only what is dissolved, but how much of it there is in a given volume — the concentration.

In this article you will see what a solution is, how concentration in moles per litre is defined, how to calculate the mass of solute needed to prepare a solution, how to make it up in the laboratory and how dilution changes the concentration but not the amount of solute. The worked examples are computed by the same engine as the Atomurus calculators.

Quick definition A solution is a homogeneous mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent. Its amount concentration is the amount of solute per volume of solution: C = n ÷ V, usually in mol/L.

§ 01 What a solution is

A solution is a mixture that looks the same everywhere, down to the scale of molecules. The substance present in the larger amount, usually a liquid, is the solvent; what is dissolved in it is the solute. When water is the solvent, the solution is called aqueous.

Salt water, sugar water and vinegar are aqueous solutions. Air is a solution of gases, and some alloys are solid solutions. In chemistry lessons, though, most solutions are solids or liquids dissolved in water, and that is the case this article follows.

§ 02 Concentration in moles per litre

Knowing what is dissolved is not enough: a spoonful of salt in a glass of water and in a bucket make very different solutions. Concentration says how much solute there is in a given volume of solution.

Chemists usually count the solute in moles, so the most common measure is the amount concentration, in moles per litre of solution: C = n ÷ V. A 0.1 mol/L solution of sodium chloride contains 0.1 mol of NaCl in every litre of solution. Concentration can also be given as mass per volume (g/L) or as a percentage, but mol/L is the one that connects directly to chemical equations.

Note that V is the volume of the solution, not of the solvent added. Dissolving a solid usually changes the volume a little, which is why solutions are made up to a mark, as the next sections show.

§ 03 How much solute to weigh

To prepare a solution you start from the volume and the concentration you want. Their product gives the amount of solute, n = C × V, and the molar mass turns that amount into the mass to weigh, m = n × M:

Example: the mass of NaCl for 250 mL of a 0.1 mol/L solution

  1. Write the volume in litres. V = 250 mL ÷ 1000 = 0.25 L
  2. Amount of solute the solution needs: n = C × V. n = 0.1 mol/L × 0.25 L = 0.025 mol
  3. Count the atoms of each element in the formula. Na: 1 · Cl: 1
  4. Look up each atomic mass in g/mol (abridged standard atomic weights, CIAAW). A(Na) = 22.99 · A(Cl) = 35.45
  5. Multiply each atomic mass by its number of atoms. 1 × 22.99 = 22.99 · 1 × 35.45 = 35.45
  6. Add the contributions: the sum is the molar mass. M = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol
  7. Mass: m = n × M. m = 0.025 mol × 58.44 g/mol = 1.461 g

Answer: m = 1.461 g

The volume must be in litres for the units to work: mol/L multiplied by L leaves mol. Using millilitres gives an answer a thousand times too large.

§ 04 Making up the solution

In the laboratory a solution of exact concentration is made in a volumetric flask, a flask with a single line on its narrow neck. The weighed solute is first dissolved in a little water, the liquid is transferred to the flask, water is added up to the mark and the flask is closed and turned over several times to mix.

Measuring 250 mL of water and then adding the solid does not work, because the final volume would no longer be 250 mL. The mark on the flask fixes the volume of the solution itself.

The calculation can also go the other way: from the mass that was dissolved and the volume of the solution you can find the concentration.

Example: 5.85 g of NaCl dissolved and made up to 500 mL. What is the concentration?

  1. Add the contributions: the sum is the molar mass. M = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol
  2. Amount of substance: n = m / M. n = 5.85 g ÷ 58.44 g/mol = 0.1001 mol
  3. Divide by the volume of the solution, in litres. C = 0.1001 mol ÷ 0.500 L = 0.2002 mol/L

Answer: C = 0.2002 mol/L

§ 05 Dilution

Adding solvent to a solution dilutes it: the volume grows while the amount of solute stays the same. Because n = C × V before and after, C₁V₁ = C₂V₂, where 1 is the solution you start from and 2 the diluted one.

Example: what volume of a 2 mol/L stock solution is needed to make 250 mL of a 0.2 mol/L solution?

  1. Diluting does not change the amount of solute, so C₁V₁ = C₂V₂. The data: C₁ = 2 mol/L · C₂ = 0.2 mol/L · V₂ = 250 mL
  2. Isolate V₁. V₁ = C₂V₂ / C₁
  3. Substitute (both volumes in the same unit). V₁ = (0.2 mol/L × 250 mL) / 2 mol/L = 25 mL

Answer: V₁ = 25 mL

Dilution is how laboratories make weaker solutions from a concentrated stock, and both volumes can be in millilitres, as long as they are in the same unit.

§ 06 Common misconceptions

"Concentration is the amount of solute"

Concentration is the amount of solute per volume of solution. Half a litre and two litres of the same 0.1 mol/L solution have the same concentration, but the larger volume contains four times as much solute.

"Dissolving 1 mol in 1 L of water gives 1 mol/L"

Not exactly: the concentration refers to the volume of the solution, which is rarely the volume of water added. That is why a solution is made up to the mark of a volumetric flask instead of being mixed with a measured litre of water.

"Diluting removes some of the solute"

Diluting adds solvent and leaves the amount of solute unchanged; only the concentration falls. This is exactly why C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ holds.

"A dilute solution is a weak solution"

Dilute and concentrated describe how much solute is present. Weak and strong, for acids and bases, describe how much of it ionizes. A dilute solution of a strong acid is still a strong acid.

§ 07 Key takeaways

  • A solution is a homogeneous mixture of a solute in a solvent; with water as solvent it is aqueous.
  • Amount concentration is C = n ÷ V, in moles per litre of solution.
  • To prepare a solution, find n = C × V (V in litres) and weigh m = n × M.
  • Make it up to the mark of a volumetric flask, so that V is the volume of the solution.
  • Dilution keeps the amount of solute: C₁V₁ = C₂V₂.
Conclusion Concentration turns a solution into a measured quantity: with C, V and the molar mass you can prepare a solution, check one and dilute it — the everyday work of any chemistry laboratory.
