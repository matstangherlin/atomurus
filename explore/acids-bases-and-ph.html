Acids, bases and pH: strong and weak acids, Ka and buffers

pH condenses the balance between hydrogen and hydroxide ions into one number. See why each unit is a factor of ten, why a weak acid has a higher pH than a strong acid of the same concentration, and how a buffer keeps the pH near its pKa.

Lemon juice, vinegar, soap, baking soda: acids and bases are everywhere, and the pH scale is how we say how acidic or basic a solution is. Behind the single number is a simple idea — how many hydrogen ions the solution holds.

In this article you will see what acids and bases are, what pH measures and why each step on the scale is a factor of ten, why a strong acid and a weak acid of the same concentration have different pH values, and how a buffer holds the pH steady. The worked examples come from the same engine as the Atomurus pH calculator, at 25 °C.

Quick definition In water, an acid increases the concentration of hydrogen ions, H⁺, and a base increases the concentration of hydroxide ions, OH⁻. The pH is pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], with [H⁺] in mol/L.

§ 01 Acids and bases

In the definition used in most school chemistry, an acid is a substance that gives hydrogen ions to water and a base is one that takes them, producing hydroxide ions. Hydrochloric acid, HCl, gives H⁺ and Cl⁻; sodium hydroxide, NaOH, gives Na⁺ and OH⁻.

Water itself takes part. A tiny fraction of its molecules always splits into H⁺ and OH⁻, and at 25 °C the product of their concentrations is fixed: Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴. More H⁺ means less OH⁻, and the other way round.

§ 02 What pH measures

Hydrogen-ion concentrations span many powers of ten, so instead of writing them out we use their logarithm. A solution with [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻³ mol/L has pH 3. Because the scale is logarithmic, each pH unit is a factor of ten: pH 3 has ten times more H⁺ than pH 4 and a hundred times more than pH 5.

The same reasoning gives the pOH, pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻], and at 25 °C the two add up to 14. A solution is neutral when [H⁺] = [OH⁻]; at 25 °C that happens at pH 7.

§ 03 Strong acids and bases

A strong acid or base ionizes completely in water. For a strong acid with one H⁺ per formula, such as HCl, [H⁺] is simply its concentration:

Example: the pH of 0.01 mol/L hydrochloric acid

  1. A strong acid ionizes completely, so [H⁺] equals its concentration. [H⁺] = 0.01 mol/L
  2. Take minus the base-10 logarithm. pH = −log₁₀(0.01) = 2.00
  3. At 25 °C, pH + pOH = 14. pOH = 14 − 2.00 = 12.00
  4. Compare with 7 (neutral within ±0.005 at 25 °C): the solution is acidic. pH = 2.00 < 7

Answer: pH = 2.00

For a strong base, the concentration gives [OH⁻] first, and the pH follows from pH + pOH = 14:

Example: the pH of 0.001 mol/L sodium hydroxide

  1. A strong base dissociates completely, so [OH⁻] equals its concentration. [OH⁻] = 0.001 mol/L
  2. Take minus the base-10 logarithm. pOH = −log₁₀(0.001) = 3.00
  3. At 25 °C, pH + pOH = 14. pH = 14 − 3.00 = 11.00
  4. Compare with 7 (neutral within ±0.005 at 25 °C): the solution is basic. pH = 11.00 > 7

Answer: pH = 11.00

§ 04 Weak acids and Ka

A weak acid ionizes only in part, and an equilibrium is set up between the acid and its ions. How far it ionizes is measured by the acid dissociation constant, Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] ÷ [HA], often given as pKa = −log₁₀ Ka. Acetic acid, the acid in vinegar, has pKa = 4.756 at 25 °C (CRC Handbook, via OpenStax).

Example: the pH of 0.10 mol/L acetic acid (pKa = 4.756 at 25 °C)

  1. Ka from the pKa. Ka = 10^(−4.756) = 1.754 × 10⁻⁵
  2. At equilibrium [H⁺] = [A⁻] = x and [HA] = C − x, so: Ka = x² ÷ (0.10 − x) = 1.754 × 10⁻⁵
  3. x is small compared with C, so x ≈ √(Ka × C); solving without the shortcut barely changes it. x ≈ √(1.754 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.10) = 1.324 × 10⁻³ · x = 1.316 × 10⁻³ mol/L
  4. Take minus the base-10 logarithm. pH = −log₁₀(1.316 × 10⁻³) = 2.88
  5. The fraction of the acid that ionized. α = x ÷ C = 1.316 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.10 = 1.3 %

Answer: pH = 2.88

At the same concentration, 0.10 mol/L, hydrochloric acid would have pH 1.00. The difference, close to two pH units, means that the acetic acid solution holds about 75 times fewer hydrogen ions; it comes from how much each acid ionizes, not from how much of it there is.

§ 05 Buffers

A buffer is a solution of a weak acid and its conjugate base — for example acetic acid and sodium acetate. Small additions of acid or base change its pH much less than they would change pure water, because the buffer’s base takes up added H⁺ and its acid replaces H⁺ that is removed.

The pH of a buffer is given, to a good approximation, by the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻] ÷ [HA]):

Example: the pH of a buffer of 0.10 mol/L acetic acid and 0.20 mol/L sodium acetate

  1. The ratio of conjugate base to acid. [A⁻]/[HA] = 0.20 ÷ 0.10 = 2
  2. Henderson–Hasselbalch. pH = 4.756 + log₁₀(2) = 4.756 + 0.301 = 5.06
  3. The ratio is between 0.1 and 10, so the equation is a good approximation: the full charge balance also gives pH 5.06. 0.1 ≤ 2 ≤ 10 ✓

Answer: pH = 5.06

A buffer works best when acid and base are present in similar amounts, roughly while the ratio [A⁻]/[HA] stays between 0.1 and 10 — that is, within about one unit of the pKa.

§ 06 Common misconceptions

"pH 7 is always neutral"

Neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻]. That happens at pH 7 only at 25 °C, where Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴. Kw grows with temperature, so pure water is still neutral when hot, but its pH is below 7.

"A strong acid is a concentrated acid"

Strong describes how completely an acid ionizes; concentrated describes how much of it is dissolved. A dilute solution of hydrochloric acid is a strong acid, and a concentrated solution of acetic acid is still a weak one.

"pH 2 is only a little more acidic than pH 3"

The scale is logarithmic: one unit means ten times the hydrogen-ion concentration. A solution at pH 2 has ten times more H⁺ than one at pH 3, and a hundred times more than one at pH 4.

"A buffer keeps the pH from changing at all"

A buffer reduces the change; it does not prevent it. Its capacity is limited, and once most of its acid or base has been used up the pH changes quickly.

§ 07 Key takeaways

  • Acids raise [H⁺] in water and bases raise [OH⁻]; at 25 °C, [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴.
  • pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]: each unit is a factor of ten, and at 25 °C pH + pOH = 14.
  • Strong acids and bases ionize completely; weak ones only in part, as measured by Ka or pKa.
  • Strength and concentration are different things.
  • A buffer of a weak acid and its conjugate base keeps the pH near pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]).
Conclusion pH condenses the balance between H⁺ and OH⁻ into one number. Knowing whether an acid is strong or weak, and how a buffer works, is what lets you predict that number — and keep it where it needs to be.
