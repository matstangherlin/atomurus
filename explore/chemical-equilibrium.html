Chemical equilibrium: reversible reactions, K and Q, and Le Chatelier’s principle

At equilibrium a reaction looks finished, but it is not: the forward and reverse reactions go on at the same rate. Learn what K and Q say about a mixture, how to find the concentrations at equilibrium and how the balance responds when conditions change.

You will learn

  • Explain why chemical equilibrium is dynamic
  • Use K and Q to predict which way a mixture reacts

Many reactions do not simply run until the reactants are used up. They go both ways at once, and after a while the mixture stops changing even though neither reaction has stopped. That state is chemical equilibrium.

In this article you will see what a reversible reaction is, why equilibrium is dynamic, how the equilibrium constant K and the reaction quotient Q tell you which way a mixture will react, how to find the concentrations at equilibrium with a simple table, and what Le Chatelier’s principle predicts when the conditions change. The numbers in the examples come from the Atomurus engines, at 25 °C.

Quick definition A system is at chemical equilibrium when the forward and reverse reactions go on at the same rate, so the concentrations no longer change. For a reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the equilibrium constant is K = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ ÷ ([A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ), with the concentrations at equilibrium.

§ 01 Reversible reactions

Some reactions go almost entirely one way: burnt methane does not turn back into methane and oxygen on its own. Many others can go in both directions. Acetic acid in water gives hydrogen ions and acetate ions, and hydrogen ions and acetate ions join again into acetic acid. Such a reaction is written with a double arrow:

CH₃COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻

The reaction to the right is the forward reaction and the one to the left the reverse reaction. Another example used in many textbooks is nitrogen dioxide, a brown gas, which joins into colourless dinitrogen tetroxide, 2 NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄. In a sealed tube the colour stops changing once both gases are present in fixed amounts.

§ 02 A dynamic balance

At the start only acetic acid is present, so only the forward reaction happens. As ions form, the reverse reaction starts and speeds up, while the forward reaction slows down because less acid is left. Eventually the two rates are equal. From then on the concentrations stay constant: in every second as many molecules ionize as ions recombine.

Nothing seems to happen, yet both reactions are still going on, which is why equilibrium is called dynamic. It can also be reached from either side: starting from the ions in the matching amounts, the reverse reaction leads to a mixture of the same composition at the same temperature.

§ 03 The equilibrium constant K

At a given temperature the concentrations at equilibrium always satisfy one relation, the equilibrium constant. The products go on top and the reactants below, each raised to its coefficient in the equation; water as the solvent, pure liquids and pure solids are left out. For acetic acid the constant is called the acid dissociation constant:

Ka = [H⁺][CH₃COO⁻] ÷ [CH₃COOH] = 1.754 × 10⁻⁵ (25 °C)

This is the value for pKa = 4.756 (CRC Handbook, via OpenStax). A large K, much greater than 1, means that the products dominate at equilibrium; a small K, like this one, means that the reactants do. K depends on the temperature, and for a given reaction only on the temperature — not on the amounts you start with.

§ 04 Concentrations at equilibrium

When K and the starting concentrations are known, the concentrations at equilibrium can be found with a table of three rows: initial, change and equilibrium, often called an ICE table. The change is written with one unknown, x, using the coefficients of the equation:

Example: an ICE table for 0.10 mol/L acetic acid: CH₃COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻

  1. Initial (I), before any ionization, in mol/L. [CH₃COOH] = 0.10 · [H⁺] ≈ 0 · [CH₃COO⁻] = 0
  2. Change (C): x mol/L of the acid ionizes. −x · +x · +x
  3. Equilibrium (E): what is left of the acid and what has formed. [CH₃COOH] = 0.10 − x · [H⁺] = x · [CH₃COO⁻] = x
  4. Put the equilibrium row into K (Ka at 25 °C, from pKa = 4.756). Ka = x² ÷ (0.10 − x) = 1.754 × 10⁻⁵
  5. Solve for the one unknown: the positive root of x² + Ka·x − Ka·C = 0. x = 1.316 × 10⁻³ mol/L
  6. The equilibrium concentrations. [CH₃COOH] = 0.09868 mol/L · [H⁺] = [CH₃COO⁻] = 1.316 × 10⁻³ mol/L

Answer: x = 1.316 × 10⁻³ mol/L · pH = 2.88

Only about 1.3 % of the acid is ionized at equilibrium, which is what makes acetic acid a weak acid. The article on acids, bases and pH reaches the same pH from the same equilibrium.

§ 05 The reaction quotient Q

The reaction quotient Q has the same expression as K but uses the concentrations at any moment, not only at equilibrium. Comparing the two tells you which way a mixture will react: if Q < K, the forward reaction goes on until Q rises to K; if Q > K, the reverse reaction does; if Q = K, the mixture is already at equilibrium.

Example: a mixture holds 0.10 mol/L acetic acid and 1.0 × 10⁻³ mol/L each of H⁺ and acetate. Which way does it react?

  1. Q has the same form as K, with the concentrations as they are now. Q = [H⁺][CH₃COO⁻] ÷ [CH₃COOH] = (1.0 × 10⁻³)² ÷ 0.10 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵
  2. Compare Q with K at the same temperature (25 °C). Q = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ < Ka = 1.754 × 10⁻⁵
  3. There is less product than at equilibrium: the net reaction goes forward (more acid ionizes) until Q = K. Q < K → CH₃COOH ⟶ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻

Answer: Q < K — forward: more acid ionizes.

§ 06 Le Chatelier’s principle

When a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it responds in the direction that partly counteracts the disturbance, until a new equilibrium is reached. This qualitative rule, Le Chatelier’s principle, covers the usual cases:

  • Concentration. Adding a reactant or removing a product makes Q smaller than K, so the forward reaction goes on. Adding acetate to acetic acid does the opposite: Q grows and the acid ionizes less, the common-ion effect behind buffers.
  • Pressure. Compressing a mixture of gases favours the side with fewer gas molecules: compressing 2 NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄ favours N₂O₄.
  • Temperature. Heating or cooling changes K itself. For a reaction that releases heat in the forward direction, heating lowers K: the sealed tube of NO₂ turns darker when warmed and paler when cooled.
  • Catalyst. A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions alike: equilibrium is reached sooner, but its position does not change.

The principle says which way the mixture moves, not how far; for that you need K, as in the common-ion case:

Example: sodium acetate is added to 0.10 mol/L acetic acid until the acetate reaches 0.10 mol/L. What happens to the ionization?

  1. Before: the acid alone, as in the ICE table. [H⁺] = 1.316 × 10⁻³ mol/L · pH = 2.88 · α = 1.3 %
  2. The added acetate makes Q larger than K: the reverse reaction goes on until Q = K again. Q > Ka → H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ ⟶ CH₃COOH
  3. At the new equilibrium, with the same Ka and both concentrations close to 0.10 mol/L: [H⁺] = Ka × [CH₃COOH] ÷ [CH₃COO⁻]. [H⁺] = 1.754 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.10 ÷ 0.10 = 1.754 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L · pH = 4.76
  4. The fraction ionized drops; K has not changed. α = 1.754 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 0.10 = 0.018 %

Answer: pH 2.88 → 4.76 · α 1.3 % → 0.018 %

§ 07 Common misconceptions

"At equilibrium the reactions have stopped"

Both reactions go on, at the same rate. The concentrations stay constant because each change is balanced by the opposite one, not because nothing is happening.

"At equilibrium reactants and products have equal concentrations"

They are constant, not equal. In 0.10 mol/L acetic acid more than 98 % of the acid stays un-ionized at equilibrium; K tells how the concentrations compare.

"Changing a concentration changes K"

Adding or removing a substance changes Q, and the mixture shifts until Q equals K again. The new equilibrium has different concentrations but the same K. For a given reaction, only the temperature changes K.

"A catalyst shifts the equilibrium towards the products"

A catalyst speeds up both directions equally. Equilibrium is reached faster, with the same composition it would have had without the catalyst.

§ 08 Key takeaways

  • In a reversible reaction the forward and reverse reactions happen at the same time.
  • At equilibrium both rates are equal and the concentrations stay constant, but the reactions go on.
  • K relates the concentrations at equilibrium and, for a given reaction, changes only with the temperature.
  • Comparing Q with K tells which way a mixture reacts: forward if Q < K, in reverse if Q > K.
  • A disturbed equilibrium shifts to partly counteract the change; a catalyst does not shift it.
Conclusion Equilibrium is a balance of two reactions, not the end of one. K says where that balance lies, Q which way a mixture is heading, and Le Chatelier’s principle how it responds when the conditions change.
