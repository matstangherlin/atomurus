What is the mole? Amount of substance, molar mass and the Avogadro constant

The mole is how chemists count particles they cannot see: a fixed number of entities, exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³. Learn how molar mass and the Avogadro constant turn a mass on a balance into moles and particles.

Atoms and molecules are far too small to count one by one, yet chemistry is about how many of them take part in a change. The mole is the unit that joins the two scales: it lets you count particles by weighing a sample on an ordinary balance.

In this article you will see what a mole is, why chemists count in moles, how molar mass turns grams into moles and back, how to get from moles to the number of particles, and what one mole of a gas looks like. Every number in the worked examples is computed by the same engine as the Atomurus calculators.

Quick definition The mole (symbol mol) is the SI unit of amount of substance. One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ elementary entities — atoms, molecules, ions or whatever entity is specified.

§ 01 What a mole is

A mole is a counting unit, in the same way that a dozen is twelve of anything. The difference is the size of the number: one mole is 6.02214076 × 10²³ entities. That number is the numerical value of the Avogadro constant, Nᴀ = 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

Since the revision of the International System of Units that took effect on 20 May 2019, this value is exact: it is fixed by definition, not measured. Before that, the mole was defined through the number of atoms in 12 grams of carbon-12, so the Avogadro constant had to be determined by experiment and carried an uncertainty.

When you use moles you must always say what you are counting. One mole of oxygen atoms (O) and one mole of oxygen molecules (O₂) are not the same amount of oxygen: the second contains twice as many atoms. Chemists write the entity next to the amount — n(O₂) = 1 mol — precisely to avoid that ambiguity.

§ 02 Why chemists count in moles

Chemical equations are written in particles. In 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, two molecules of hydrogen react with one molecule of oxygen. In the laboratory, however, nobody handles single molecules: we weigh grams and measure millilitres.

The mole solves that mismatch. Because the same number of entities is in one mole of anything, the coefficients of an equation can be read directly in moles: 2 mol of H₂ react with 1 mol of O₂ to give 2 mol of H₂O. The equation keeps its meaning, and the amounts become quantities you can actually weigh.

§ 03 Molar mass

The molar mass M of a substance is the mass of one mole of it, usually given in grams per mole (g/mol). For an element, it has the same number as its standard atomic weight: the abridged standard atomic weight of oxygen is 15.999, so oxygen atoms have M ≈ 15.999 g/mol.

For a compound you add up the molar masses of the atoms in its formula, each multiplied by the number of times it appears. The subscript tells you that number: CO₂ has one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.

Example: the molar mass of CO₂

  1. Count the atoms of each element in the formula. C: 1 · O: 2
  2. Look up each atomic mass in g/mol (abridged standard atomic weights, CIAAW). A(C) = 12.011 · A(O) = 15.999
  3. Multiply each atomic mass by its number of atoms. 1 × 12.011 = 12.011 · 2 × 15.999 = 31.998
  4. Add the contributions: the sum is the molar mass. M = 12.011 + 31.998 = 44.009 g/mol

Answer: M = 44.009 g/mol

Different substances have very different molar masses, which is why one mole of water and one mole of sugar have the same number of molecules but not the same mass.

§ 04 From mass to moles and back

Molar mass is the conversion factor between mass and amount. Dividing a mass by the molar mass gives the amount of substance; multiplying an amount by the molar mass gives the mass:

n = m ÷ M  and  m = n × M

Example: how many moles are there in 36 g of water?

  1. Count the atoms of each element in the formula. H: 2 · O: 1
  2. Look up each atomic mass in g/mol (abridged standard atomic weights, CIAAW). A(H) = 1.008 · A(O) = 15.999
  3. Multiply each atomic mass by its number of atoms. 2 × 1.008 = 2.016 · 1 × 15.999 = 15.999
  4. Add the contributions: the sum is the molar mass. M = 2.016 + 15.999 = 18.015 g/mol
  5. Amount of substance: n = m / M. n = 36 g ÷ 18.015 g/mol = 1.998 mol

Answer: n = 1.998 mol

A quick check is to look at the units: grams divided by grams per mole leaves moles. If the units do not cancel, the calculation is set up the wrong way round.

§ 05 From moles to the number of particles

Once you know the amount of substance, the Avogadro constant gives the number of entities: N = n × Nᴀ. Because Nᴀ is exact, the precision of the answer comes only from the measured mass and the molar mass.

Example: how many molecules are there in 36 g of water?

  1. The amount of substance, from the previous example. n = 36 g ÷ 18.015 g/mol = 1.998 mol
  2. Multiply by the Avogadro constant. N = 1.998 mol × 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.203 × 10²⁴ molecules

Answer: N = 1.203 × 10²⁴ molecules

Numbers this large are the reason the mole exists: no one could write or use them every time a reaction is described.

§ 06 One mole of a gas

For gases there is a further shortcut. An ideal gas obeys PV = nRT, so at a given temperature and pressure one mole always occupies the same volume, whatever the gas. That volume is not a constant to memorise: it is RT/P, and it depends on the conditions you choose.

Example: the volume of 1 mol of an ideal gas at 0 °C (273.15 K) and 101.325 kPa

  1. Write the data in SI units (Pa, m³, mol, K). P = 101325 Pa · n = 1 mol · T = 273.15 K
  2. Isolate V in PV = nRT. V = nRT / P
  3. Substitute, with R = 8.3145 J·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹ (CODATA 2018). V = (1 mol × 8.3145 × 273.15 K) / 101325 Pa = 0.02241 m³
  4. Convert the result to the unit asked. V = 0.02241 m³ = 22.41 L

Answer: V = 22.41 L

At a pressure of 100 kPa instead of 101.325 kPa the same calculation gives a slightly larger volume, which is why a molar volume must always be quoted with its temperature and pressure. Real gases depart a little from the ideal model, most of all at high pressure and low temperature.

§ 07 Common misconceptions

"A mole is an amount of mass"

No: a mole is an amount of substance, a number of entities. Mass enters only through the molar mass, which is different for every substance. One mole of hydrogen molecules and one mole of gold atoms contain the same number of particles but have very different masses.

"Molar mass and molecular mass are the same thing"

They have the same number but not the same meaning. The mass of one water molecule is about 18 daltons; the molar mass of water is about 18 grams per mole, the mass of 6.02214076 × 10²³ molecules. One describes a single particle, the other a mole of them.

"The Avogadro number is still being measured"

Not any more. Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the Avogadro constant is fixed exactly at 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Experiments now measure other quantities, such as the mass of an atom, using that fixed value.

"One mole of any gas weighs the same"

At the same temperature and pressure one mole of any ideal gas has the same volume, not the same mass. One mole of helium has a mass of about 4 g and one mole of carbon dioxide about 44 g, in the same volume.

§ 08 Key takeaways

  • The mole is the SI unit of amount of substance: exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities.
  • Always state the entity you count: one mole of O atoms is not one mole of O₂ molecules.
  • Molar mass, in g/mol, converts mass into moles (n = m ÷ M) and moles into mass (m = n × M).
  • The Avogadro constant converts moles into a number of particles (N = n × Nᴀ).
  • The volume of one mole of an ideal gas is RT/P: quote it with its temperature and pressure.
Conclusion The mole lets a balance stand in for a particle counter. With molar mass and the Avogadro constant you can move freely between grams, moles and particles — the three ways chemists describe how much of a substance takes part in a reaction.
